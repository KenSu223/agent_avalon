\section{What this means for memory design}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The bottleneck is where the risk lives, and the failure is deletion.} At the word budgets
a strong compactor normally runs under it loses essentially nothing (retention
\RetentionBThreeHundred{} at 300 words, \RetentionBSixHundred{} at 600); degradation appears only
once the budget is genuinely binding, so a comfortable compression ratio is not safety. And when it
does appear, it is deletion: only \ChangedCount{} of \NCodedCases{} coded item-checkpoints
(\ChangedRate{}) are changes to a different value. The failure practitioners worry about --- a
constraint quietly rewritten into a plausible default --- is not what a strong compactor does. That
matters for mitigation, because a check comparing a stored value against an expected range will never
fire: there is nothing left to compare, and detecting the loss requires knowing what \emph{used to
be} in memory, which is exactly what rolling rewrite destroys.

\paragraph{Keep the sources, not just the summary.} The largest effect we measured is the gap between
rebuilding memory from retained raw sessions and editing the previous summary in place. A budget
forces an omission at each step either way; recursion is what makes it permanent, so a system that
keeps its sessions and periodically rebuilds avoids the accumulation, at the cost of a longer input.
Where keeping the sources is impossible, the anchoring rules already shipping in production prompts
recover a comparable amount at no extra budget --- though they make the model overshoot the length
limit far more often, and a prompt rule is a request the model may decline. Choosing \emph{what} to
preserve by surprise buys nothing over choosing at random.

Finally, the worry that compaction smooths a user profile toward an average one did not reproduce
(\S\ref{sec:prefs}): on this evidence the thing to engineer against is plain deletion, not
homogenisation.
