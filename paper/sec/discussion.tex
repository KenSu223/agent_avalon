\section{What this means for memory design}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The bottleneck is where the risk lives, and the failure is deletion.} At the budgets a
strong compactor normally runs under it loses essentially nothing (retention
\RetentionBThreeHundred{} at 300 words, \RetentionBSixHundred{} at 600); degradation appears only once
the budget is binding, so a comfortable compression ratio is not safety. And when it appears it is
deletion: \ChangedCount{} of \NCodedCases{} coded item-checkpoints (\ChangedRate{}) are changes to a
different value. A check comparing a stored value against an expected range will therefore never fire
--- there is nothing left to compare --- and detecting the loss requires knowing what \emph{used to
be} in memory, which is exactly what rolling rewrite destroys.

\paragraph{Keep the sources, not just the summary.} The largest effect we measured is the gap between
rebuilding memory from retained sessions and editing the previous summary in place. A budget forces an
omission either way; recursion makes it permanent, so a system that keeps its sessions and periodically
rebuilds avoids the accumulation, at the cost of a longer input.
Where keeping the sources is impossible, the anchoring rules already shipping in production prompts
recover a comparable amount at the same storage cap --- though at several times the generation cost,
and a prompt rule is a request the model may decline. Choosing \emph{what} to
preserve by surprise buys nothing over choosing at random.

Finally, the worry that compaction smooths a user profile toward an average one did not reproduce
(\S\ref{sec:prefs}): on this evidence the thing to engineer against is plain deletion, not
homogenisation.
